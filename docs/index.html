Abstract

Kaizen is continuous improvement in small steps against a written standard. This paper is a descriptive case study of kaizen in an agent software factory, a system in which AI coding agents write, test and ship software under rules people set. The factory studied is an agent team run with Agent Scrum. We define a method-independent improvement loop and map Agent Scrum and nine other published accounts onto it. In 16 days to 5 November 2026 the team filed 417 retrospective issues; of 20 sampled done issues, 18 named a merged change and none recorded a measured effect. On 6 November the team added an evaluation step: each retrospective issue states a prediction, and a later wrap records a verdict. In the next 17 days, 303 of 323 retrospective issues (94%) carried a prediction and 129 of 156 due predictions (83%) received a verdict: 95 held, 18 did not, 16 could not be measured. All 129 verdicts ended in keep; none in reverse. The agents that write a prediction also grade it, and we did not audit whether the measurements ran, so the figures show records, not effects. The prediction on authorship failures could not be tested.

Keywordskaizen, agent software factory, compound engineering, Experience Factory, andon, jidoka, PDCA, retrospectives, AI coding agents, Agent Scrum, case study

1Introduction

Kaizen is the practice by which a Toyota plant improves its own work: small changes, proposed mostly by the people doing the work, each tested against a written standard and kept if it helps [7]. In 2026 several teams began to call their setups for AI coding agents software factories (Section 6). Most try to make the factory better at its own work, a practice some call compound engineering. This paper asks what kaizen and the disciplines around it, quality management and software process improvement, say about that effort, and what one factory's records show about it.

The paper is a case study of one factory, not a test of a general claim. Its reference case is an agent team run with Agent Scrum, a sprint method with a committed backlog, a Definition of Done and a retrospective at every wrap [2]. We use the team because we can read its records; the loop of Section 5 is stated so that other methods can be mapped onto it.

The Agent Scrum paper, published on 28 October 2026, called the retrospective the method's weakest part: 119 of 159 retrospective actions counted from sprint reports were open on that date [2]. In the 16 days from 20 October, when the team began labeling retrospective issues, to 5 November, the agents filed 355 of them on the infrastructure repository and 62 on the product repository.

Why the paper is written. We write it to share what one team learned, so that others can use and correct it, and to improve the team's own implementation: explaining a practice to a reader exposes its gaps, and working in the open invites correction. Writing version 0.4 exposed that the team recorded fixes but not their effects; the team added an evaluation step on 6 November (Section 8.3), and Section 9.3 reports its first 17 days. Reading related work exposed a second gap: the team's lessons files (Markdown notes in which agents write down a diagnosis and a rule after a failure) record diagnoses and rules but not predictions or outcomes (Section 6.2).

Our contributions are (1) a short account of the quality-management and software-process ideas that bear on agent factories; (2) a method-independent improvement loop and a comparison of ten entries against it; (3) a separation of an improvement's stages, proposed, implemented, closed and verified, with counts for one team; (4) two cases followed from filing to intervention; and (5) a test of an evaluation step, with predictions stated in advance and results over 17 days.

2Background

2.1 Predictions, standards and kaizen

Walter Shewhart introduced the control chart, a plot of a measurement over time with limits that separate ordinary variation from a cause worth finding, and set out its theory in 1931 [37]. In 1939 he described quality control as a cycle of specification, production and inspection [3]. W. Edwards Deming taught statistical quality control in Japan from 1950, and Japanese engineers turned it into the PDCA cycle: Plan a change, Do it on a small scale, Check the result, Act by adopting or abandoning it. Deming later taught the PDSA cycle, with Study in place of Check, because "check" suggested inspection where he meant learning from a prediction [4, 29]. The difference matters here: in PDSA one states in advance what the change should do, so the result can refute it. Total Quality Management (TQM) is a broader, later label for organization-wide quality management [29, 30]. This paper uses specific mechanisms from it and does not claim to apply TQM as a whole (Section 10).

The Toyota Production System (TPS) [5, 6] rests on just-in-time production and jidoka (Section 2.2). Standardized work fixes the pace, the sequence and the work in progress for each job [5, 6]. Kaizen is continuous improvement in small steps against that standard [7]. Kaizen needs a standard: without one there is nothing to measure a change against and nothing to update when the change works.

2.2 Containing and preventing

Jidoka means that a machine or a worker stops when a defect is found, rather than passing defective work downstream where it costs more to fix. The andon is the signal for it, often a cord along the line: a worker who sees a problem pulls it, a light shows the station and a team leader comes [5, 6]. Andon contains a defect; it does not by itself prevent the next one. Prevention is the job of the improvement that follows (Figure B1 in Appendix B shows the two side by side). Poka-yoke, mistake-proofing, is a method developed by Shigeo Shingo [38]: a device that makes an error impossible (a fixture that accepts a part only the right way around) or detects it at once and stops or warns [5, 38]. It works without anyone having to remember a rule. A written instruction does not meet this definition, because it depends on the reader obeying it.

2.3 Software process improvement

At NASA's Software Engineering Laboratory, Victor Basili and colleagues developed the Quality Improvement Paradigm: characterize the environment, set measurable goals, choose a process, execute it, analyze the results, and package what was learned [31, 32]. The Experience Factory is the organization that does the packaging: a group separate from the projects that turns their data and lessons into models, guidelines and tools the next project uses [31]. Basili compared the paradigm with PDCA and TQM [32]; Watts Humphrey's process-maturity work also drew on Deming [33]. Three features bear on agent teams: the experience organization is separate from the project organization; work starts from measurable goals, as the Goal/Question/Metric approach turns each goal into questions and measures before the work begins, the same requirement as the prediction in PDSA [40]; and what is packaged is reusable experience, kept with its context [31].

The Experience Factory is the nearest earlier model of a retrospective backlog; making lessons survive the end of a project is the problem an agent team faces at every context reset. The reference case has the third feature (rules, gates and briefs that hold lessons), part of the first (a separate repository and backlog for the team's own process) and mostly lacks the second (Section 9.1).

2.4 Compound engineering

Compound engineering is a term from Every. Kieran Klaassen defined it in 2025 as "building self-improving development systems where each iteration makes the next one faster, safer, and better" [8]. Every's later guide states the principle as "each unit of engineering work should make subsequent units easier" [9]. Its loop is plan, work, review, compound: the lessons are written into the instructions, tools and checks the agents use next time. The guide gives two allocation rules: planning and review take 80 percent of the time within a piece of work, and half of an engineer's time goes to improving the system that builds the features [9]. Reflexion (2023) is a related research line in which a language-model agent writes verbal reflections on its failures and reads them on the next attempt [34].

2.5 Lineage: influence or analogy

It is tempting to say that compound engineering descends from TQM. The evidence supports three kinds of claim, which we keep apart: documented influence (a later author says the earlier work shaped theirs), plausible indirect influence (the ideas were in wide circulation, but no citation links them) and functional analogy (two practices do similar jobs and may have arisen independently). Most links among the older work are documented [10, 11, 32]; the link from Deming to TPS is only plausible, as we found no source in which its designers credit him [5, 6]. For compound engineering the two Every texts we read on 5 November 2026 cite none of DevOps, Lean, agile retrospectives, the Experience Factory or Reflexion [8, 9]. The links from DevOps, Lean and agile retrospectives are plausible indirect influence, because those ideas were common in software practice by 2025; the links from the Experience Factory and Reflexion are analogy (Table B1). The absence of citations is weak evidence against descent, because practitioner writing often omits sources. This paper uses the older results as a source of questions, not as results that transfer. Appendix B.2 gives each link.

3Method

Design. A descriptive single-case study of one team, with no comparison group. We chose the case because we could read its records, not because it is typical.

Window and cutoff. 20 October 2026, when the from-retro label was first used, to 5 November 2026 at 16:00 Mountain Standard Time (UTC−7), the time zone used for times of day in Sections 1 to 9.2, in Appendix B and in Figures 1 and 4. Section 9.3 states its own conventions.

Sources. Issues labeled from-retro on the infrastructure repository and (counted on the morning of 5 November, not recomputed) on the product repository; sprint reports and the sprint index; the nightly CI sweep reports; commit history and closing comments; and the CI service's record of the daily full test run (details in Appendix B.3). The infrastructure repository is private, so the issues and pull requests this paper cites are labeled C1, C2 and so on, with their dates, times and counts as recorded.

Stages of an improvement. We count four stages separately. Proposed: an issue was filed. Implemented: a merged change is named in the issue or cites it. Closed: the issue was closed, as done or as not planned. Verified: a measurement shows the intended effect. Proposed and closed are counted for every issue; implemented and verified are estimated from the audit sample below. Closure is an administrative state: it can come from a merge, from a grooming pass that finds the problem already fixed, or from a decision not to act.

Statistics and audit. Medians and 90th percentiles are of hours from filing to closing, by close reason; the 90th percentile is the value at zero-based index ⌊0.9(n−1)⌋ of the sorted list, not the nearest-rank convention. Sprints are not independent observations: consecutive sprints share agents, backlog and standards. For the audit we drew 20 done issues at random, with a fixed seed, and checked each for a merged change (a commit on the main branch that cites it, or one named in its closing comment) but not for its effect. Intervals for the audit sample are exact (Clopper–Pearson) 95% binomial intervals; the nominal intervals in Table 4 are Wilson score intervals.

Backlog passes and case selection. We identify backlog passes from bursts of closures and report them separately (Table 3). The two cases in Section 9.2 were chosen because each has a measurement or a recorded count before its intervention; only the CI case also has one after. That choice biases the cases toward improvements whose effect can be measured.

4The reference case

The team is described in two earlier working notes [1, 2]; nothing here depends on how it is hosted. The engineering agent acts as product owner and plans sprints. It is one of the team's long-running agents, each of which has a role (engineering, research, operations and so on) and keeps a session across days; sprint agents, by contrast, are short-lived. A scrum-team agent runs each sprint and starts short-lived developer and reviewer agents for each task, from a written brief that may contain a stop rule, a condition under which the agent must stop and report. Reviews use two reviewer agents from different model vendors. Sprints lasted two hours until 3 November and three hours, about eight a day, since then. Work comes from two repositories: the product (a web application) and the infrastructure repository, which holds the agents' instructions, scripts, tests and gates (checks in continuous integration, CI, that must pass). Every sprint ends with a wrap that writes a report and a retrospective, and each retrospective finding ends in a filed issue, a mitigation already applied, or a stated reason not to act [2, §5.11]. The paper uses Agent Scrum's vocabulary [2, §6].

Three features of this deployment shape what follows; they describe this team, not agents in general. First, sprint agents start from a fresh context: a developer agent knows only its brief and the repository, and the scrum-team agent's context is cleared at each wrap. A lesson that is not written into a file is not available to the next task. Second, sprints run around the clock, so a change merged at 02:00 is used by 08:00. Third, improvement work and product work draw on the same rate-limited model capacity.

5A method-independent improvement loop

To compare factories that use different methods, we need a description of improvement that does not assume sprints, retrospectives or a product owner. We use seven steps, drawn from PDSA and the Quality Improvement Paradigm:

  1. Observe. A problem is detected, inside the line (a failed check) or outside it (an idea from elsewhere).
  2. Contain. The defective unit is stopped before it moves on.
  3. Investigate. The cause is found and its cost measured.
  4. Select. Among all proposed changes, some are chosen and the rest are deferred or rejected.
  5. Change. The chosen change is made to a standard: a rule, a gate, a test, a script, a brief.
  6. Evaluate. The effect is measured against a prediction made before the change.
  7. Retain or reverse. The change stays in the standard, or is taken out.

Steps 1 and 2 are andon and jidoka. Steps 3 to 7 are kaizen. This split is this paper's construction, not a TPS definition: Toyota describes jidoka as both stopping at an abnormality and preventing its recurrence [39], so at Toyota jidoka also reaches into steps 3 to 7. Any factory can be mapped onto these steps; nothing requires a ceremony. The loop has two inputs: problems seen inside the line, and ideas from outside, such as a scheduled news scan (Appendix B.4). The mapping of TPS tools onto AI-assisted software work is not new: a Lean Enterprise Institute article of November 2025 (an interview with Fabrice Bernhard of Theodo, written by an AI model from the transcript, as the page states) maps jidoka, andon, poka-yoke and kaizen onto it and treats automated tests as the andon [27]. This paper adds the separation of lineage from analogy, the common criteria of Table 2 and the stage-by-stage counts of Section 9.

6.2 Agents that learn from outcomes

Anthropic's Dreams reads a memory store and past session transcripts and writes a new, reorganized store, leaving the input unchanged [14]. Cognition's Agent Memory Repo keeps an agent's memory in linked Markdown files and runs a periodic agent that adds patterns found across sessions [15]. Both reorganize what an agent remembers; the reference case's document passes read documents, not transcripts (Section 8.2).

The closest prior work is Self-Improvement Memory (SIM) by Yu, Xia, Pan and colleagues, on continued automated optimization of an agent harness [41]. For each selected edit, SIM records its rationale, the predicted and observed task outcomes and the context, grouped by root cause, and the optimizer reads the whole record before proposing the next edit. One domain is scored by a model judge whose bias the authors acknowledge. Over ten paired coding runs, the full record improved the result in nine, by 2.36 points on average (paired p = 0.003); the other domains have one run per condition. A record of root causes alone had mixed effects, from 1.82 points below to 2.40 points above the harness-only baseline. We have not reproduced these results. Our evaluation step has the same shape (a prediction before the change, an observed outcome after it), and the team's lessons files resemble the root-cause-only condition. But SIM optimizes a harness automatically and scores against held-out tasks, whereas our fixes are chosen by agents and graded against a measure the filer chose, with no independent scorer. We report records of the step, not improvement, so we cannot claim SIM's gain.

Bessant, Caffyn and Gallagher describe stages by which organizations develop continuous improvement as a routine [42]. Their separation of collecting suggestions, running a process and sustaining the capability matches this paper's separation of filing, implementing and verifying; their findings concern people, not agents. The Scrum retrospective [16] and the blameless postmortem [12] are functional analogues of the kaizen review; we found no source documenting a direct line from either. This paper's addition to Agent Scrum's rule that every finding ends somewhere [2] is to count each stage after the issue separately.

7A planned allocation of capacity, and the retrospective channel

The 70/30 split. At each sprint planning the product owner agent records its approximate product share in the sprint index ("~70% product"). The record began with sprint 394 on 3 November 2026. In the 15 sprints from 394 to 408 the recorded share ranged from 60% to 90%, with a median of 70% and a mean of 73% (Appendix A). These numbers are a planning policy, not a measurement of where capacity went. The recorded value is the planned share, written by the agent that sets it, so the policy holding over 15 sprints shows only that the planner followed its own rule. The unit is not stated (story points or a count of stories), and neither is model capacity consumed. And membership in the infrastructure repository is not the same as improvement work. Published shares (20% for non-functional work in The DevOps Handbook [11], about half in Every's guide [9], about a fifth of engineering time on cleanup at OpenAI [19]) are single-organization claims, not evidence of an optimum.

A rule for rejecting the 30%. We state the policy so that it can be rejected. The argument for a fixed share is that standards must follow changes in models and tools; it supports continuing maintenance, not a particular share. A test needs four things the records do not yet have: the capacity actually spent on each side, a stable identity for each kind of finding, a count of eligible tasks per sprint (so that recurrence per eligible task can be computed) and a stated unit for product work. With those, we propose a noninferiority test of a lower improvement share. The comparison is a prior period at the current share against a period at the lower share, each over at least four weeks, with a lag of two weeks before each period (so that improvements funded earlier do not count). Sprints are not independent, so the intervals use weekly blocks or a cluster-robust method. The lower share is judged noninferior if the upper 95% confidence bound of the relative rise in recurrence per eligible task is below a margin of 10%, and the lower 95% bound of the relative change in merged product points per sprint is above −5% (a fall of less than 5%; a rise is expected). A higher share is judged worthwhile if the upper 95% bound of the relative change in recurrence is below −10% and the lower 95% bound of the relative change in product points is above −5%. The lag, exposure, margins and interval method are our proposal and must be fixed before the lower-share period starts. A short comparison favoring a lower share is preliminary evidence, not grounds to reject the policy.

The retrospective channel. The retrospective is not the team's andon cord. Andon is steps 1 and 2 of the loop: a failed gate, a blocking reviewer or a task that hits its stop rule contains a defect and leaves a record. The retrospective at the wrap is step 3 onward: it reads that record and files proposals. In the eight days after the Agent Scrum paper, retrospective filing changed from a section written by the scrum-team agent to a channel any agent writes to. A retrospective issue is filed by the agent that saw the problem, in the sprint in which it saw it, and records the measured cost (for example "two extra two-seat review rounds", C15 in Appendix B.5; a same-day cycle is C20 and C21 there) and a proposed fix. It waits for planning; it does not stop the sprint.

A description of this channel is not a finding. A testable claim is this: after a countermeasure that has been verified, the rate at which the same finding recurs, per eligible task, should fall, and the fall should outweigh the cost of filing, grooming and reviewing the proposals. Evidence against it would be a recurrence rate that stays flat after verified countermeasures, or a handling cost that grows faster than recurrences fall.

8The loop applied to itself

A suggestion system can collect proposals faster than it acts on them. The team met two forms of this, and responded with an evaluation step.

8.1 Grooming the retrospective backlog

By 1 November the open backlog was over 300 issues, and planning could not read it all. The product owner agent groomed it in three passes. On 31 October a triage closed 61 issues as not planned, each naming the issue it duplicates. On 3 November, at four sprint plannings, 92 were closed as done from lists of candidates, each naming the commit or repository state that shows the problem already fixed (seven more closed as done that day through an ordinary merge). On 5 November 23 were closed as not planned, each with a one-line reason. Together the passes closed 176 of the 324 retrospective issues closed in the window (54%; Table 3 and Figure 3).

8.2 A document check with no applier

Since 9 September a nightly pass has read the team's documents and proposed corrections where a claim no longer matched the repository. By 5 November it had written 316 proposals in 34 review notes. It had a detector and a writer but no automatic applier: a person or an agent had to read each note and make the change. Some were applied (81 commits cite the pass). A check on 5 November found 98 proposals whose quoted stale sentence was still in the target document. Not every one is a failure: a reader may have judged a proposal wrong, and the notes do not record such rejections.

Diagnosis: the loop had steps 1 and 3 but no reliable step 5, no recorded step 4 (which proposals were rejected, and why) and no step 6. The proposed evaluation (C2) gives each proposal a stable identity and a lifecycle in which "applied" means a merged change plus a check that the target now holds the new text. None of it has shipped.

8.3 Adding the evaluation step

Section 9.1 finds the evaluation step mostly missing. On 6 November the team scheduled a change to add it (C22), and this paper's public release was held so that a later version could report the result. The change has four parts:

  1. A prediction on each fix. A retrospective filing that proposes a fix carries a line of the form Predicts: what changes — by how much — measured how.
  2. A verdict at a later wrap. The wrap lists retrospective issues whose prediction is due and posts one comment on each, beginning kaizen-eval: held | not-held | unmeasurable — keep | reverse. Keep or reverse is loop step 7.
  3. A record of authorship failures. A pull request opened from a remote session (a task run in the model vendor's cloud instead of locally) is labeled as such, and a failure is recorded in a comment before the commits are re-authored, since that removes the evidence.
  4. One count. A single command counts predictions filed, verdicts recorded, held against not held, and authorship failures per remote-session pull request (Appendix B.6).

We state the predictions here, before any data, so that a later version can test them. The window runs from the day the change lands to about 20 November. P1 to P3 are directional predictions with thresholds; P4 is a planned descriptive analysis. The items are as written in v0.5.

  • P1. At least half of the retrospective fixes filed in the window carry a prediction. Baseline: no sampled issue recorded one.
  • P2. At least half of the predictions that fall due in the window receive a verdict. Baseline: 0 of 20 sampled done issues recorded a measured effect (Section 9.1).
  • P3. Authorship failures fall below 2 in 20 eligible remote-session pull requests. Baseline: 18 failures in 20 eligible pull requests before 4 November (Section 9.2).
  • P4. We make no prediction about the share of verdicts that are "held". A share near 100% would suggest that the predictions are set too easily to fail; a share near zero, that most fixes do not work as intended. Either is a finding.

If P1 or P2 fails, the step exists in name only, as the document check of Section 8.2 had a detector and no applier. How firmly the predictions were stated in advance. The text of v0.5 is a file written on 6 November 2026 at 14:02 MST, before the instruction change merged that evening at 18:40. The first backup copy of the author's notes that contains it was taken at 03:17 MST on 7 November (10:17 UTC); the earliest prediction in the data is from 7 November 02:36 UTC and the earliest verdict from 11 November. The backup is 7 hours 41 minutes later than the first prediction, so it does not show that the text came first; only the file time does, and a file time can be changed. There is no external registry, so a reader can inspect the claim only through those times.

9Results

9.1 Implemented and verified

Of the 20 done issues sampled, 18 named a merged change, either a commit on the main branch citing the issue (14) or a change named in the closing comment (4). Two closed with no change named. None of the 20 recorded a measurement showing the change had its intended effect. With 20 issues these proportions are imprecise: 18 of 20 gives an exact 95% interval of 68% to 99%, and 0 of 20 gives 0% to 17%, so the sample is consistent with as many as about one done issue in six recording a measured effect, but it contains none. Most done issues were implemented and verification is rare. That is the gap the evaluation step of Figure 2 marks.

Of the 91 issues closed as not planned, 61 are duplicates closed in the 31 October pass, each naming the issue it duplicates. The other 30, including the 23 of the 5 November pass, were not coded by reason, so we cannot say how many were judged not worth doing and how many were already moot.

9.2 Two cases: one measured effect, one awaiting evaluation

The CI gate: an observed latency improvement. At 18:52 on 27 October the retrospective of sprint 318 filed that a pull request's gate had needed three attempts of about 55 minutes each, timing out on test suites the change did not touch (C3). By 05:24 on 28 October the team had raised the per-suite time limits where a loaded runner had been measured (C4) and capped the job at 90 minutes (C5). That removed the timeouts and nothing else: the slowest runner's median stayed between 51 and 72 minutes through 2 November. At 07:03 on 1 November the product owner agent measured the cause: all twelve recent runs had executed all 344 suites for one-line edits to data and instruction files (C7). Three pull requests made a push run only the suites that read the changed files, plus a smoke set (a small fixed group run on every push), and added a full run of every suite scheduled once a day; they merged between 09:44 on 1 November and 02:49 on 2 November (C8, C9, C10). Appendix B.5 gives the sequence.

What this shows (Figure 4) is a latency improvement for a push: the slowest runner's daily median fell from between 51 and 72 minutes (median 59.5 over eight days) to 4 or 5, and the spread between runners closed. The comparison mixes two changes: a smaller workload and a lighter runner load. A push now runs only the suites that read the changed files, so the gate does less work; and the earlier days had loaded runners (the cause behind C4), which the later days may not have had. The records cannot separate a smaller workload from lighter runner load, so the paper claims only that the push gate got faster and now does less work. The daily full run is a reference series for the full workload under different load, not a controlled comparison: its gate job made five attempts on three days and took 47, 13, 49, 24 and 17 minutes, and the two successful attempts took 24 and 17.

The case does not show that quality held. Running fewer suites per push trades coverage for speed, and the records do not measure the cost: how often the full run catches a failure the push runs missed, or how many such failures reached the main branch. Three of the five full-run attempts failed. We did not determine whether any was a defect the push runs had missed, which is the measurement this case lacks. The two first fixes were correct for the timeouts and did not move the median; it moved only after the cause had been measured (C7).

Commit authorship: recurrent non-compliance, then an intervention awaiting evaluation. Tasks run as remote sessions open pull requests whose commits carry the model vendor's default author and whose descriptions end with a generated footer, against the team's rule that nothing is attributed to the model. The stakeholder set that rule on 19 October 2026 as a personal preference; the paper does not argue for it. Compliance is easy to observe, which makes it a useful case (Figure 5). The retrospective of sprint 330 filed it at 18:55 on 28 October: six of eight such pull requests needed their commits re-authored by hand (C11). The fix was an instruction in the task brief. Five more filings followed, three of them before any mechanism: seven of seven (C12), every such pull request in one sprint (C13) and five of five (C14). The three filings that give counts (C11, C12, C14) report 18 failures in 20 eligible pull requests. Two mechanisms came later: a command that strips the footer before the first review (merged 23:36 on 2 November, C16) and a launch-tool change that writes a commit-identity section into every brief that lacks one (merged 02:21 on 4 November, C17). One recurrence followed: the footer came back whenever a pull request's description was edited (C18, fixed the same evening, C19). Appendix B.5 lists all six filings, including C15, which gives no count.

The case shows recurrent non-compliance with an instruction, followed by mechanisms whose effect is not yet known. Neither mechanism is mistake-proofing in the sense of Section 2.2. The identity section assures that the instruction is present, not that it is obeyed, and footer stripping repairs the output after the fact; neither refuses a wrongly authored commit, which a check that rejected it before review would. At the cutoff the footer stripping had run for under three days and the identity section for under two, and one recurrence (C18) came after them. The deciding measure is failures per eligible pull request before and after 4 November, over at least a week. Eligible pull requests are not marked in the records, and re-authoring rewrites the commits that would show a failure, so the rate has to be counted from the sprint reports, which we have not done.

The records point to a first cause: no remote session ran. Remote sessions had been funded by a cloud-credit grant that expired on 5 November [1]; from then they would draw on the same usage limits as the rest of the team. The same day the team set remote sessions to stop launching above 85% of the weekly usage limit and moved them onto the long-running agents' account, so they no longer drew on separate capacity. All 129 end in keep and none in reverse, including the 18 not-held verdicts and the 16 unmeasurable ones. The comments we read describe partial results (for example, a failure rate that fell but did not reach zero), so a keep after a not-held verdict can mean the fix helped in part. We did not read all 18, or check whether any not-held verdict led to a follow-up task. Step 7 produced no reversal in 17 days, so the reverse branch of step 7 has not been used.

10Where the analogy fails

  • Quality defined by the customer. TQM starts from what the customer values. Almost all of the reference case's improvement work is internal (faster gates, cleaner commits, better briefs), and we did not measure customer-visible outcomes such as defects found by users or time from request to release.
  • The whole system. Deming warned against improving one part at the expense of the whole. Faster CI by running fewer suites is a local optimization until its effect on escaped defects is measured.
  • Variation. Shewhart's central idea, separating ordinary variation from causes worth acting on, is absent: a single bad sprint can produce a retrospective issue, and no control limits decide which findings merit action.
  • Leadership and incentives. At Toyota, management commitment and the absence of blame are what make workers pull the cord. Agents are not punished or rewarded, so "proposals are not punished" does not describe a human incentive here. What makes agents file is the method's rule that every finding ends somewhere.
  • The cost of standards. Each improvement adds a rule, gate or instruction, and standards that grow without pruning become their own source of defects. TPS keeps standardized work short. The team has no measure of the cost of its accumulated rules.
  • Fresh-context agents. The claim that lessons survive only in files applies to this deployment's sprint agents; long-running agents keep some context, and the model brings knowledge no file holds.

11Limitations

  • One team, short windows. The label dates from 20 October, the split from 3 November, and the evaluation step covers 6 to 22 November. Sprints are not independent, and the periods do not establish a trend.
  • Closure is not improvement, and the cases were selected. Grooming closed many issues against one change, and the sample shows implementation, not effect. Not-planned closures other than the 61 duplicates were not coded by reason. The two cases were chosen because they had measurements, and the CI reference series is five attempts over three days, so it is not a controlled comparison.
  • Self-set and self-reported inputs. The planner sets and records the split, and there is no independent measure of where capacity went (Section 7). Table 2 rests on short public accounts, several read through secondary sources and one from abstracts, and is a qualitative map.
  • The grader is not independent, and the markers are records. The agents that write a prediction also run its measure and write the verdict, and the team has no grader outside what they can change. A held share therefore shows that agents judged their own predictions to hold; it covers only the evaluated fraction of the due predictions (61% held of all due, 74% of verdicts, 84% of decidable ones). A marker shows that a prediction, a verdict or a keep was written, not that the measurement ran, and we did not audit a sample of verdicts for whether the measure ran or its value matches the verdict. Self-Improvement Memory [41] scores predicted against observed outcomes on held-out tasks; this deployment has no such scorer.
  • Adoption, not effect. P1 and P2 test whether the step ran, and their thresholds were low and set by us. The step landed for every wrap at once, so there is no concurrent control. The 16 "unmeasurable" verdicts are outside the decidable held share (84%), and every verdict ended in keep, so the reverse branch of step 7 has not been used.
  • P3 untested. No eligible remote-session pull request was identified in the tracked sources, so the authorship mechanisms of Section 9.2 are still unevaluated.
  • Counts depend on the reading. Predictions are read from issue bodies as at extraction, without edit history; the count moved between readings (Section 9.3); creation filters are in UTC; one verdict per issue is counted; issue state is as at extraction; and the intervals are nominal and ignore clustering.
  • Author's position. The author runs the reference case, and the analysis was drafted with AI assistance; the de-identified export lets an outside reader check every count except the keep and reverse counts, which need the forge.

12Conclusion

Original cohort, to 5 November. In the reference case the early steps of the improvement loop were in place and the evaluation step was mostly missing. Detection and containment are well developed, and proposals follow in volume once every finding has to end somewhere: 355 retrospective issues on the infrastructure repository and 62 on the product repository in 16 days. Of 20 sampled issues closed as done, 18 named a merged change and none recorded an effect; the one large latency gain has an unmeasured quality cost, and the authorship mechanisms were less than three days old at the cutoff. The 70/30 split is a policy that no data yet supports or refutes. Follow-up cohort, 6 to 22 November. The team added a prediction at filing and a verdict at a later wrap. In this deployment, over 17 days, 303 of 323 retrospective issues carried a prediction, 129 of 156 due predictions received a verdict, and 95 of 113 decidable verdicts held. All 129 verdicts ended in keep. These figures show that predictions and verdicts were recorded, not that the fixes work (Section 11). The prediction about authorship failures could not be tested. What to build next: audit a sample of verdicts and give each prediction a check the proposing agent cannot grade; classify what the full CI run catches that push runs miss; act on each verdict; measure recurrence per eligible task; code why proposals are rejected; and measure the cost of the standards.

B.3 Method detail

The nightly CI sweep reports record, for the 24 hours before 03:30 each day, the number of gate runs and the median gate time per CI runner. The CI service's record of the daily full run gives the start and end time of each attempt of its gate job. Reopened issues count as open. The from-retro label was first used on 20 October. Product-repository counts were taken on the morning of 5 November and were not recomputed, so their ages and passes were not computed. The bursts of closures identified as grooming passes were found by reading closing comments where many issues closed within minutes of each other.

Context for the volume of work between 29 October and 5 November: the team merged 392 pull requests on the product and 290 on the infrastructure repository, and 315 issues were filed on the infrastructure repository, 173 of them (55%) from retrospectives. Issue counts do not measure effort or value, since a one-line fix and a redesign are each one issue; they say that most of what the infrastructure backlog received that week came from the team's own retrospectives.

B.4 Ideas from outside the team

Two routes bring ideas in. A scheduled job scans AI engineering news on Monday and Thursday mornings and writes what applies into a report and, where actionable, an issue. The stakeholder also forwards individual posts, and the research agent answers within the day with a short report and, if the answer is yes, a proposal to the product owner agent. On 4 November 2026 a post by Dave Kiss pointed at the AGENTS.md file of the uv project, which tells its agents: "DO NOT leak our conversation, prompt, or iteration history into code comments, pull request descriptions, or other maintainer-facing prose. Write for readers who have not seen our conversation" [13]. On 5 November a pattern search over the team's script and hook directories found 581 lines in 150 files that matched a pattern for review-round history. The count is a heuristic: the matched lines were not classified, and some may be comments that still explain the code. A rule, an exactly specified census, a parser-based guard per language and a pilot were filed as an issue on 5 November (C1). An earlier post by the same author, on 31 October, about a run-and-verify skill, was answered the same day as applying to the product, not the scripts, and went to the product backlog.

B.5 Case chronologies

CI gate. At 18:52 on 27 October the retrospective of sprint 318 filed the three 55-minute attempts (C3). By 05:24 on 28 October the per-suite time limits had been raised (C4) and the job capped at 90 minutes (C5). On 29 October the next retrospective found the next symptom, a merge waiter (a script that waits for the gate to finish and then merges) that gave up after 45 minutes while the gate still ran (C6). At 07:03 on 1 November the cause was measured: all twelve recent runs had executed all 344 suites for one-line edits to data and instruction files (C7). The three pull requests that made a push run only the suites that read the changed files, plus a smoke set, and added the daily full run, merged at 09:44 and 21:48 on 1 November and 02:49 on 2 November (C8, C9, C10).

Commit authorship. The sprint 330 filing at 18:55 on 28 October reported six of eight (C11); the fix was an instruction in the brief. Five more filings followed: seven of seven in sprint 333 (C12, 29 October); every such pull request in sprint 342, plus a merge that brought back three re-authored commits (C13, 29 October); five of five in sprint 356 with one wasted review round (C14, 30 October); on 3 November, with no failure count, two extra two-seat review rounds in one sprint because a re-authored head (a pull request whose commits were rewritten after review) needs a new review (C15); and on 4 November at 16:24 the footer coming back when a description was edited (C18). The footer-stripping command merged at 23:36 on 2 November (C16); the launch-tool change at 02:21 on 4 November (C17); the fix for the recurrence at 20:43 on 4 November (C19).

A same-day cycle. At 06:53 on 1 November a retrospective filed that the hand-written time on the sprint's run log had run up to 14 minutes ahead of the clock in two sprints in a row (C20). The fix, a clock read in place of the writer's estimate, closed at 16:06 the same day (C21): 9 hours from filing to closing, against the 32-hour median for done issues. We did not measure how typical this is, or whether the fix held.

B.6 Evaluation analysis detail

The count command does not compute due-date denominators or the planned-end reading; those come from the analysis script. The median gap between filing and due date is 6.4 days. An earlier hand-built version of the retrospective-only count over-counted evaluated issues (160 instead of 129) by treating issues with other comments as evaluated; the script replaces it, and its totals equal the count command's (95 + 18 + 16 = 129). The wrap's rule for verdicts: it lists issues with a Predicts: line whose due date is on or before today and that have no kaizen-eval: comment, skips one whose fix has not merged, and posts at most five verdicts per wrap, oldest first. The due date is a calendar date chosen by the filer, not a sprint count. A comment that starts with the marker but names no valid verdict would count as evaluated with no verdict ("malformed"); there are none.
